### Notes and observations:

This chapter is in the middle of the book and in the thick of things. The placement of this chapter is noteworthy since this chapter addresses one of the central themes of this book - that of identity. On the surface of it, this chapter is about a garden - actually a backyard when Chee discovers it for the first time, when he inspects an apartment, in Brooklyn, in December 1995.

#### Carving a garden from a backyard ####
Maybe it is because Chee sees some potential in the backyard (and maybe in himself, as someone tending to that piece of land that’d eventually be transformed into a garden), that he seems to be fallen in love with it then. He explains, in great detail, how human psychology works, when we want something. We only look at the positive and ignore the negatives, or act as if the negatives do not exist. A bit like falling in love where you see only the good things about a person and ignore the bad. You know the bad exists, infact, you know exactly where it is, but you totally sideline it. Here’s an example of one such line:

> *“The winter mud, the dead grass, the snow, these all seem like lies after the strange vision I’ve just had.”*
>

Chee goes into great detail explaining his garden exploits. This is not just storytelling but also fact-telling, because as readers, we learn so many interesting bits and techniques of gardening while reading this chapter.

The entire chapter is an exercise in personification - at times, of the garden or of the rose plants inhabiting it (Sometimes though, we thought, he overdid it). He talks of the roses like they were his pets (or even, friends) that need care and nurturing and were living, almost having their own personalities. He addresses the plants with their common names (”Joseph’s coat”, “Therese Bugnet”) almost making them sound like humans. He even describes them as having indigestion, fainting, quivering, etc.

The chapter begins with Chee in the company of a real estate broker, overlooking an apartment in Brooklyn, in 1995. He likes the apartment, it has a garden but the rent is cheap and Chee wants to understand why.

> *“She says nothing else, and the silence is out of a horror film, a silence that tells the audience, I will later discover, that anonymous, unspeakable murders have happened here.”*
>

This is an excellent description of the word silence. The way he describes it by saying a ‘silence that tells’ making it an irony cause silence is quiet but here it is telling.

> *“The only explanation is that it was some gift of the apartment’s, an otherwise unmystical, unmagical place.”*
>

This is an interesting way to describe the house he is going to live in, using the “un” prefixed words. By doing this he is indirectly implying that all that remained apart from the apartment (which is the garden), was mystical and magical.

As the author slowly starts to carve a garden out of a backyard, he begins with first “undoing the mistakes of previous owners”:

> *“I tear up the stone walk. It occupies patches of ground feasting on sunlight, a feast of no use to a rock. “*
>

Here the verb “tearing” which is usually associated with paper (or some soft textured material - like linen/cloth) is being used for stone walk. When we “tear” things, we do so intentionally, and with ease. It is possible that the author wants to subtly stress that the surety of his intentions made this otherwise physically daunting task easier for him.

> *“Occasionally a sock or panty falls into my garden. No one ever comes to ask for them, and I do not ever know whose these are, and inevitably I throw whatever the stray item is away.”*
>

In the above lines, instead of directly saying he was lonely or he had not made friends with the neighbours, the uses the excellent technique to visually describe something to convey the message (of loneliness)

> *“I follow a line of greenery out along a spit of sand and rock to a sandbar, where I come across a sea rose perched on, or really around, a granite boulder. The roots wrap the boulder like the ribbon on a present and probe for chinks. Erosion has taken away the ground around it and the rock has tumbled into the beach, so the rose is growing at an angle, reaching for the sun, new buds flourishing. They lift along the side of the rock and over it, as big as a single bed. The ocean and the rose compete in a slow race to break the boulder apart, though it looks as if the rose flew down to grab the boulder, and having caught it, won’t fly off and won’t let go. You might think a rose was something delicate, but you’d be looking in the wrong place.”*
>

This is a great piece of writing because it brings life and drama and action to something very mundane, very ordinary. This is the magic of a great writer, to bring all his imagination into describing something so ordinary. He has used several figures of speech including personification, simile. He has created vivid, beautiful imagery where he has compared the rose around the boulder to ribbon a present.

#### Shedding skins ####
The author then mentions about his stay at Sweetbriar, Virginia, where he has gone to work on his first novel. He is to stay there for five weeks and ends up writing around 120 pages - a substantial amount of his first novel. On the morning of his departure from Sweetbriar, the following account:

> *“On the morning of my departure, I discover a black-snake skin, shed whole and without a tear. Its previous owner had spent the previous week sunning itself on the fence near my studio and left its coat across the walkway to my door. I imagine the snake using the thorns to shed its skin, but I see no holes in it anywhere when I hold it up to the light. Instead, it glows in the sun, the scales light up, the blue sky shows through the holes for its mouth and eyes”*
>

 This section of the story forms like a prelude to the climax - it is so beautifully positioned and described. The author mentions that he “does well” with his novel writing at Sweetbriar. Notice how the shed skin is perfect, flawless - with no holes. The snake skin, here, is an analogy for a new identity that the author has discovered on his stay there and while he does not say much about himself there, he uses the analogy of the shed snake skin to reveal how he is pleased with his own transformation as a writer. That this happens at the end of the trip is perfect timing, because it is the culmination but also the start of something new. Later it is revealed that the whole incident is considered to be a good omen by the indigenous people there.

> *“Perhaps it was the gift of the snake. The lesson for me at least — and this I think of as the gift of the garden, learned every year I lived in that apartment: you can lose more than you thought possible and still grow back, stronger than anyone imagined.”*
>

Now here, the snake skin incident becomes a metaphor. The author now compares the shedding of the snake skin to his experience with the garden - just like the snake, who shed its skin (lost) but gained a new identity as a result of the loss, a similar thing happened with his garden. When he went back to it after his writing trip, fearing he would lose his roses as he had incorrectly pruned them more than needed, he found them instead to be huge and stronger than he would have imagined.

> *“I had come to this garden much like what I found in it. I was a mess, a disaster in need of a reckoning. That backyard was my perfect mirror, and the dream of the garden was in its own way a dream of myself. I arrived there after many years of self-abandonment, surely only that I did not know myself, but certain that I needed to believe I had a future…”*
>

#### The Mirror ####
Towards the end of the essay, he compares the backyard to a mirror. Notice the word “backyard” and not “Garden” (because just a sentence ago, he refers to it as a “garden”). The backyard was how he found it, the “garden” is what he made of it. The garden eventually becomes a “messenger” which tells him that there’s more to come - possibly hinting of a successful career as a writer (As we know, Chee wrote his first novel here). 

He also thinks of himself as someone who “pushed” the garden (or its inhabitants) hard to grow, cutting them, pruning them more than needed etc. He calls himself the tormentor, and while they did grow, so did he, in the process. In this ending, he brings together beautifully how this piece of land became his reflection and also they both became teachers of each other - while Chee pushed the garden to its limits, the garden instead taught him patience and that something he can be (as Chee says, “I was not their gardener. They were mine”).
